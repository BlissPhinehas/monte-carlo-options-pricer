% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Monte Carlo simulation is a foundational tool in quantitative finance,
widely used to price derivatives whose payoffs depend on complex stochastic
processes. This paper examines the performance of three Monte Carlo estimators
for pricing European and Asian options under the Black-Scholes framework:
naive simulation, antithetic variates, and control variates. We implement all
three methods from scratch in C++ using Geometric Brownian Motion to simulate
price paths, and evaluate each estimator's convergence rate, variance, and
pricing accuracy relative to the analytical Black-Scholes solution. Our results
demonstrate that antithetic variates reduce estimator variance by approximately
29\% over naive simulation, while control variates reduce variance by over 99\%,
recovering the analytical price with negligible error. We further apply these
techniques to Asian options, for which no closed-form solution exists,
demonstrating Monte Carlo's unique value in pricing path-dependent derivatives.
\end{abstract}

% ── 1. Introduction ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

The pricing of financial derivatives is one of the central problems in
quantitative finance. While simple European options admit closed-form solutions
under the Black-Scholes model, the vast majority of real-world derivatives;
including path-dependent options, basket options, and exotic structures --- do
not. Monte Carlo simulation provides a general and flexible framework for
pricing such instruments by directly simulating the stochastic evolution of
the underlying asset and computing the expected discounted payoff under the
risk-neutral measure.

Despite its generality, naive Monte Carlo suffers from slow convergence: the
standard error of the estimator decreases at a rate of $O(1/\sqrt{N})$, where
$N$ is the number of simulated paths. Halving the standard error requires
quadrupling the number of paths. This computational cost motivates the study
of variance reduction techniques, methods that reduce estimator variance
without increasing the number of simulations.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A rigorous mathematical treatment of three Monte Carlo estimators:
    naive simulation, antithetic variates, and control variates.
    \item A complete C++ implementation of each estimator, built on an exact
    discretisation of Geometric Brownian Motion.
    \item Empirical comparison of convergence rates and pricing errors across
    all three methods for both European and Asian options.
    \item Visualization of simulated price paths, estimator convergence, and
    payoff distributions.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 establishes
the mathematical background, including the Black-Scholes model, Geometric
Brownian Motion, and risk-neutral pricing. Section 3 derives each variance
reduction technique. Section 4 describes the implementation. Section 5
presents empirical results. Section 6 analyses the tradeoffs between methods,
and Section 7 concludes.

% ── 2. Mathematical Background ────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Mathematical Background}

\subsection{The Black-Scholes Model}

The Black-Scholes model assumes that the price of the underlying asset
$S_t$ follows a stochastic differential equation (SDE) of the form:

\begin{equation}
    dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t
\end{equation}

where $\mu$ is the drift, $\sigma > 0$ is the volatility, and $W_t$ is a
standard Brownian motion. Under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$,
the drift is replaced by the risk-free rate $r$:

\begin{equation}
    dS_t = r S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Geometric Brownian Motion}

Applying It\^{o}'s lemma to $\ln S_t$, the exact solution to the SDE is:

\begin{equation}
    S_T = S_0 \exp\left[\left(r - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T
    + \sigma W_T^{\mathbb{Q}}\right]
\end{equation}

For simulation purposes, we discretise over $n$ time steps of size
$\Delta t = T/n$:

\begin{equation}
    S_{t + \Delta t} = S_t \exp\left[\left(r - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)
    \Delta t + \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} \, Z\right]
\end{equation}

where $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. This is the \textit{exact} solution at
each step, not an Euler approximation, so it introduces no discretisation
error.

\subsection{Risk-Neutral Pricing}

Under the risk-neutral measure, the price of any derivative with payoff
$\Lambda(S_T)$ is:

\begin{equation}
    V_0 = e^{-rT} \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\Lambda(S_T)]
\end{equation}

For a European call with strike $K$, the payoff is
$\Lambda(S_T) = \max(S_T - K, 0)$, giving the Black-Scholes formula:

\begin{equation}
    C = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2)
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{align}
    d_1 &= \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} \\
    d_2 &= d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}
\end{align}

and $N(\cdot)$ is the standard normal CDF. For Asian options, the payoff
depends on the arithmetic average price:

\begin{equation}
    \Lambda = \max\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} S_{t_i} - K,\ 0\right)
\end{equation}

No closed-form solution exists for this payoff under arithmetic averaging,
which is precisely where Monte Carlo becomes essential.

% ── 3. Variance Reduction Techniques ─────────────────────────────────────
\section{Variance Reduction Techniques}

\subsection{Naive Monte Carlo}

The naive Monte Carlo estimator approximates $V_0$ by simulating $N$
independent paths and averaging the discounted payoffs:

\begin{equation}
    \hat{V}_{\text{naive}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} e^{-rT} \Lambda(S_T^{(i)})
\end{equation}

By the Law of Large Numbers, $\hat{V}_{\text{naive}} \to V_0$ as
$N \to \infty$. The standard error is:

\begin{equation}
    \text{SE} = \frac{\sigma_Y}{\sqrt{N}}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma_Y^2 = \text{Var}(e^{-rT}\Lambda(S_T))$. This $O(1/\sqrt{N})$
convergence rate is the fundamental limitation of naive Monte Carlo.

\subsection{Antithetic Variates}

The antithetic variates method exploits the symmetry of the normal
distribution. For every path driven by $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, we
generate a paired path driven by $-Z$. Since $-Z$ has the same distribution
as $Z$, both paths are valid samples, but they are negatively correlated.

Let $Y^+$ and $Y^-$ be the payoffs from the original and antithetic paths.
The antithetic estimator is:

\begin{equation}
    \hat{V}_{\text{anti}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N/2}
    \frac{Y_i^+ + Y_i^-}{2}
\end{equation}

The variance of each averaged pair is:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Var}\left(\frac{Y^+ + Y^-}{2}\right) =
    \frac{\text{Var}(Y^+) + \text{Var}(Y^-) + 2\text{Cov}(Y^+, Y^-)}{4}
\end{equation}

Since $\text{Cov}(Y^+, Y^-) < 0$ for convex payoffs, the variance is
strictly less than $\text{Var}(Y^+)/2$. No additional simulations are
required, the antithetic path is free.

\subsection{Control Variates}

The control variate method introduces a correlated variable $X$ whose
expectation $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is known analytically. The control variate
estimator is:

\begin{equation}
    \hat{V}_{\text{cv}} = \hat{V}_{\text{naive}} - c({\hat{X} - \mathbb{E}[X]})
\end{equation}

This estimator is unbiased for any value of $c$, since
$\mathbb{E}[\hat{X}] = \mathbb{E}[X]$. The optimal coefficient that
minimises variance is:

\begin{equation}
    c^* = \frac{\text{Cov}(Y, X)}{\text{Var}(X)}
\end{equation}

The resulting variance is:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Var}(\hat{V}_{\text{cv}}) = \text{Var}(Y)(1 - \rho^2_{XY})
\end{equation}

where $\rho_{XY}$ is the correlation between $Y$ and $X$. In our
implementation, we use the Black-Scholes call price as the control variate.
Since the MC payoff and the BS call payoff are highly correlated
($\rho \approx 1$), the variance collapses dramatically.

% ── 4. Implementation ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Implementation}

The engine is implemented in C++17 and organised as a static library with
four modules:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{BlackScholes}: Computes the analytical call and put prices
    using the closed-form Black-Scholes formula. Used both as a benchmark
    and as the control variate.
    \item \texttt{PathSimulator}: Generates GBM price paths using the exact
    log-normal discretisation. Supports both standard and antithetic path
    generation.
    \item \texttt{EuropeanPricer}: Prices European calls and puts using all
    three estimator modes.
    \item \texttt{AsianPricer}: Prices Asian average-price options using
    antithetic variates.
\end{itemize}

The build system uses CMake 3.28 and the test suite uses Google Test 1.14
with 17 unit tests covering correctness of the analytical pricer,
path simulation properties, and convergence of all three estimators.
The random number generator is a 64-bit Mersenne Twister
(\texttt{std::mt19937\_64}) seeded for reproducibility.

% ── 6. Analysis ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Variance Reduction Effectiveness}

Antithetic variates reduced the standard error from 0.0465 to 0.0329,
a reduction of approximately 29\%. This matches the theoretical prediction:
for convex payoffs under GBM, the negative correlation between antithetic
paths is strong, and the variance reduction is substantial at essentially
zero additional computational cost.

Control variates achieved a far more dramatic reduction, collapsing the
standard error to near zero. This is because the Monte Carlo call payoff
and the Black-Scholes call payoff are extremely highly correlated;
both depend on the same terminal stock price $S_T$. With $\rho \approx 1$,
the factor $(1 - \rho^2)$ in the variance formula approaches zero.

\subsection{When Each Method Wins}

Naive MC is suitable when simplicity is paramount and computational
resources are abundant. Antithetic variates are always preferable to
naive MC for convex payoffs; they are free in terms of extra paths
and consistently reduce variance. Control variates are the most powerful
technique when a correlated analytical benchmark exists. For Asian options,
no such benchmark exists, so we rely on antithetic variates alone.

\subsection{Convergence Rate}

All three estimators converge at the theoretical $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ rate,
as shown in the convergence panel of Figure \ref{fig:plot}. The control
variate estimator converges to the exact price, while naive and antithetic
estimators converge to slightly different values depending on the random
seed, with the antithetic estimator showing a tighter band around the
true price.

% ── 7. Conclusion ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented a comparative study of three Monte Carlo estimators
for option pricing under the Black-Scholes framework. We demonstrated
that variance reduction techniques, particularly control variates,
can dramatically improve the accuracy and efficiency of Monte Carlo
pricing engines without increasing computational cost proportionally.

The control variate method, exploiting the known analytical Black-Scholes
price as a benchmark, reduced estimator variance by over 99\% in our
experiments. Antithetic variates provided a consistent 29\% reduction at
zero additional cost. For path-dependent instruments such as Asian options,
where no analytical solution exists, Monte Carlo with antithetic variates
remains a practical and accurate pricing tool.

Future work could explore Quasi-Monte Carlo methods, which replace
pseudo-random sequences with low-discrepancy sequences to achieve
convergence rates approaching $O(1/N)$, as well as stratified sampling
and importance sampling for further variance reduction.
